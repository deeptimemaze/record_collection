# Crate

A small installable web app for keeping track of where your records and CDs are stored. Import your collection from Discogs, place each record in a storage location, and search by artist or title to find out where it sits.

## Features

- Import a Discogs CSV export. Choose which formats to keep: LP/12"/10", 7", shellac, CD, cassette.
- Place each record in a storage location by section and position, counting from 1.
- See each location as a wireframe 3D drawing, with the number of records in every section, shelf or drawer. Tap a drawer to pull it out and see its records standing front to back.
- Give each record a genre and a free-text comment, and put it in one of three selections. Hold its card in any list: after half a second it moves to the next selection, and it keeps stepping every 0.8 seconds until you let go: selection A (135° hatching), selection B (45°), selection C (cross-hatching), then back to not selected. A short note and one to three vibration pulses (a longer buzz for none) say where it landed. On the full card, **Selection** picks None, A, B or C directly. A record is in one selection at a time. Each selection has its own colour: neon lime, hot tile orange and violet (dark olive, burnt orange and deep violet in light mode). The tabs and the full card's control use the same colours. The hatching runs behind the album art from the card's left edge to the art's right edge, and the card's outline, the art's border and the hatching's right edge take the same colour. Selected records have their own tab, whose icon is a card with the hatching; at the top it has **All** plus one tab per selection, each with its hatch and count. The Selected page lists records in storage order: by location (in the Data tab's order), then section, then position, so records that stand together are listed together; records that aren't placed come last, alphabetically. With A, B or C open, a strip in its colour names it (**Selection A · 8 records**) with an **Empty** button and a trash can: it asks first, takes every record out of that selection (where they're stored and everything else stays), and a note afterwards has **Undo**. All has no Empty button. A record you move to another selection while one of those tabs is open stays in the list, faded, until you change tab. The CSV export writes A, B or C in the Selected column; 1, 2 or 3 and **Yes** from older exports are read too (Yes as selection A). **Selected only** under Pick uses all three selections.
- Place several selected records at once: on the Selected tab, tap the album art of each record in the order they should stand. Each one turns neon azure, shows its number (1, 2, 3...) instead of the art, and moves to the top of the list in that order; tapping it again takes it out. A bar above the tabs shows how many are numbered, with **Clear** and **Place**. **Place** asks for the location, the section, shelf or drawer, and where number 1 goes; the rest follow it in order, records already there move along to make room, and records stored elsewhere move from there. It warns when that is more than the section holds. The records stay selected, and the numbers are forgotten when you leave the Selected tab. Ratings come from Discogs. Once placed, the records leave their selection and drop out of the list.
- The phone's **back** button steps back through the app instead of closing it: an open card or sheet closes first, then each page you came through (the bottom tabs, Explore's tabs, Graph or Quiz) in turn. At the first page, back shows "Press back again to close Crate"; a second press within three seconds closes the app.
- On a full card, swipe left for the next record and right for the previous one, in the order of the list you opened it from (Records, Selected, Explore and so on); on Records and Selected it follows the whole list, past the records shown before **Show more**. The card follows your finger with the next one sliding in alongside, like a carousel: let go past about a quarter of the way, or flick, and it moves on; otherwise it springs back. At the first or last record the card only stretches a little and springs back. On a computer the left and right arrow keys do the same.
- Share a record: the share icon (three connected dots) in the top-right corner of the album art on the full card opens a share sheet. Title, artist, year, format and the Discogs link are always included; you choose whether to add a picture card with the album cover (in Crate's colours), label, catalogue number and original year, genre and tempo, your comment, and plays. A preview shows the text and the picture. **Share…** opens the phone's own share sheet (Messages, Mail, WhatsApp...), and **Copy text** copies the text. Where the record is stored is never shared. The choices are remembered on the phone.
- Search by artist, title, comment, Discogs notes, label, catalogue number, folder or Discogs style. Accents are ignored, so "sigur ros" finds Sigur Rós and "einsturzende" finds Einstürzende.
- **Filter** on Records opens a full-screen filter page with its own ✕: Placement (not placed, or a storage location), Format, Speed, Genre, Tempo, Decade and Label. Tap as many chips as you like: several in one group mean either (LP or 7"), and the groups narrow each other down (LP and Jazz). Each chip shows how many records it would give with the rest of your choices, chips that would give none are faded, and the button at the bottom shows how many match (**Show 42**). Decade counts from the first release, or from this pressing, as you choose. Label shows the six most common labels; **All labels ›** opens a list of every label with a search, and spellings of one label (Capitol, Capitol Records) count as one. Back on Records, the Filter button shows how many filters are on, and the filters in use sit under the search as chips: tap one to drop it, or **Clear all**. Search works on top of the filters. Selected, Pick and Timeline keep their format and genre menus, which show how many records each choice has. A record that isn't placed has a quiet grey dashed **Not placed** tag; colour is kept for what you've chosen.
- **Explore** tab, with a joined tab bar for Pick, Listened, Stats, Timeline and Live: pick a random record (filter by format and genre, selected only, only placed, favour unplayed) and keep a play-next list. The same buttons (**Played today**, **Add to play next**, **Remove last play**) are under the pick, under each record in Play next, and on the record sheet.
- Listened: what you've played over the last 30 days, 12 months or all time. It shows totals and your current day streak, an activity grid with a square per day, the most-played records, and top artists, genres, formats and weekdays, followed by the full history day by day. Every **Played today** counts once per day and is kept, so the history builds up as you play. Buttons that are on (**✓ Played today**, **✓ In play next**) are lime with a tick; tapping a lit **Played today** takes today's play back. **Remove last play** takes back the newest play. When you mark a record in Play next as played it leaves the queue, and an **Undo** note puts it back.
- Speed: vinyl cards show 33⅓, 45 or 78 rpm. Discogs leaves the speed out of its CSV export, so the app fills it in where the format makes it clear (LP, 7", shellac). **Data → Fetch details from Discogs** looks up each release on the public Discogs API for the exact speed, genres, styles and country, starting with records whose speed is unclear. It runs at about 24 records a minute and keeps going where it left off. Records without a genre get the first Discogs genre. The Format filters have a Speed group.
- Tempo (BPM) for 12" singles and EPs: **Data → Fetch tempos** looks up a suggested BPM for each track, from AcousticBrainz (measured from audio, through MusicBrainz) and from Deezer (by artist and title). About half of the 12"s are found, and a suggestion can be half or double the real tempo or belong to another mix, so the record sheet shows each track's values with **×½**, **×2**, **Confirm** and **Use** buttons. A big **TAP** button measures the tempo as you tap along to the record; it saves itself a moment after you stop. **Listen** measures it from the microphone instead: hold the phone near the speaker while the record plays. A 12-band visualizer shows what it hears, the estimate updates every second, and it stops by itself once the beat is steady (usually 8–10 seconds, at most 20) and saves the result. Nothing is recorded or kept, and the microphone is released when it stops or the record sheet closes. It works on beat-driven music; it can land on half or double the tempo, which ×½ and ×2 fix. The microphone only works when the app is opened over https, as on GitHub Pages. Cards show the BPM in an azure chip in the top right corner (dashed, with a ~, while it's only a suggestion); the record sheet has the same chip beside the title, with the Select chip below it, the Format filters have a Tempo group (90–100, 120–125 and so on), and Stats shows the spread. Deezer is called with JSONP, its only way to work from a web page.
- Record sheet: tap a record to open it. It starts with the album art in large (the 500 px image from the Cover Art Archive, when covers are on), then the title, artist and year line, the location tag and the BPM chip, as on the cards. Location, section or shelf and position are at the bottom, below the Discogs details. Plays, tempo, selection, genre and comment are kept as soon as you change them; **Save location** is only for where the record is stored, and **Close** closes the sheet.
- Discogs details: the record sheet shows the speed (in the line under the artist), format tags (reissue, limited, mono, gatefold, coloured vinyl and so on), label and catalogue number, country, genres and styles, your rating, condition, where and when you bought it and the price, your Discogs folder and notes, and a link to the release on Discogs.
- Original release year: the Released year from Discogs is the year of your pressing. **Data → Fetch original years** looks up when each album first came out on MusicBrainz (the release group's first release date). Where it is known, the card shows that year in **bold**, instead of the pressing year, and the Timeline and the decade stats use it. It is saved with each record and included in the CSV export as the `Original release` column.
- **Artists** (under Explore): a graph around one artist, label or genre, built from your records. An artist sits in the centre with its labels and genres around it; tap any node to make it the centre, so you can browse from an artist to its label, to the other artists on that label, and on. Artists are lime dots (magenta in light mode), labels grey squares, genres hexagons. A label or genre with many artists shows eleven at a time; **+N** shows the next ones. It reopens where you left it, even after the app has been closed (the first time, on your most-collected artist). On a full card, the small wireframe ball under the BPM chip (twelve nodes and their links, turning slowly, about once every ten seconds) opens the graph on that record's artist: the ball explodes, its nodes shoot out across the screen trailing lines back to the icon, then swing in and land where the graph's real nodes sit; leftover nodes fade on the way. Tap during it to skip to the end. With reduced motion turned on, the ball stands still and the graph just opens. The trail at the top takes you back, the search finds an artist, label or genre, and the button under the graph opens Records with that artist searched, or that label or genre filtered. **Samples** come from **Data → Fetch samples**, which reads MusicBrainz's "samples material" links for each album's tracks, both ways: what your records sample, and who sampled them. One pressing of each album is looked up (found through its Discogs link, otherwise by artist and title), hip hop, electronic and dance first; expect a few hours for a big collection, and it keeps going where it left off. Samples are cyan arrows pointing from the original to the record that used it, and the panel names the tracks ("Kraftwerk “Trans-Europe Express” → Afrika Bambaataa & Soulsonic Force “Planet Rock”"); an artist you don't own records by is a cyan ring. To fit the phone, each album keeps at most 24 samples and 12 "sampled by", your own artists first. The quiz asks about samples too ("Whose track is that?"). Coverage is good for well-known hip hop and electronic records and thin elsewhere.
**Special events** are ticket-shaped. Festivals, benefit concerts and award shows come from MusicBrainz with the artist lookup (artists looked up before get one short extra pass), and what an artist "took part in" (Live Aid, a Eurovision contest) comes from Wikidata when places are connected (**Connect places and events**). Festival days and stages count as one event; tours and single concerts at a venue are left out; and a MusicBrainz event only shows when Wikidata knows it, it's a benefit or tribute, or at least two of your artists were there. **Your own concerts** from the setlist.fm import are lime tickets: acts at one venue within three days make one night or festival, shown when you saw two or more there and at least one is in your collection; acts you saw but don't own records by are white hollow rings. The quiz uses events too ("Took part in Live Aid", "You saw them at …").
**Producers, remixers and featured artists** come from Discogs credits, read by **Data → Fetch details from Discogs** (records looked up before this get one more pass). Producers and remixers are orange dashed links, and one with no records of their own is an orange ring; featured artists (Q-Tip on De La Soul, Ice Cube on Public Enemy) join as collaborations. Around a producer you see every artist they worked on in your collection, and the panel lists which records ("Hank Shocklee produced Fear Of A Black Planet"). Executive, film and video producers, A&R and sleeve credits are left out, and so are producer credits on DVDs and Blu-rays; self-produced records add no link. A producer's name also works in the Records search, and the quiz can ask about "Produced by" groups once a producer has worked with three of your artists.
**Data → Fetch artist connections** adds band members, groups, collaborations and where each artist comes from, looked up on MusicBrainz most-collected artists first (through one of your releases when it's linked to Discogs, otherwise by name, and only when exactly one artist has that name). A group then shows its members, and a member with no records of their own (Chuck D, Flavor Flav) appears as a hollow lime ring, so members connect through their group. Places are white diamonds. After a lookup the app connects them through Wikidata (**Connect places** runs by itself and takes a minute or so): each place gets the chain above it up to the country, plus the regions it's on, so Nassau County and Amityville both lead to **Long Island**, and from there to New York. An artist links to its own town, its regions and its state; a place shows the places above it, the places inside it, and every artist in your collection from anywhere inside it. Only the lines to the centre are drawn, to keep it readable. The first page around an artist mixes up to six members with its place, labels and genre. MusicBrainz is often busy, so the lookup waits and retries on its own; expect roughly an artist every five to ten seconds, a few hours for a big collection. It keeps going where it left off, and the results take about 1 MB on the phone for 1,500 artists.
- **Stars** (Explore → Artists, next to Graph, Map and Quiz): the whole collection at once. Every artist is a star, sized by its records, grouped in a cluster per genre. Pinch or scroll to zoom (names appear as you zoom in); tap a star to light up its connections (members, collaborators, producers, samples, influences, similar artists, and artists from the same town or event) and open it in the graph. **Whole sky** zooms back out.
- **Wander** (Graph): the graph moves on by itself every four seconds to a neighbour it hasn't just visited, artists more often than labels or genres, like a radio. Tap anything to stop.
- More links in the graph: **collaborations in names** ("Eminem Featuring Dr. Dre" links Eminem and Dr. Dre; "A & B" only splits when A or B has records of their own, so Earth, Wind & Fire stays whole); from Wikidata, with Places and events, **where an artist was born or founded** (used when MusicBrainz has only a country or nothing, and it gets regions and map coordinates like any place), **influenced by** (violet arrows from the influence), **awards** (stars), and **years active** (the panel says "active 1975–1991", and the decade an artist started is a node: the 1980s gathers everyone who started then); and **similar artists** from ListenBrainz (**Data → Lookups → Similar artists**, about a second an artist): ones you own are linked with faint lime dots, and the panel suggests the ones you don't, to dig for next.
- **Map** (Explore → Artists, the third mode next to Graph and Quiz): **Where they're from** puts your artists where they started (lime dots, sized by how many artists), and **Where you saw them** puts your concerts in their cities (pink dots, sized by nights). Drag to pan, pinch or scroll to zoom, or use World, Europe, N. America and All dots; dots close together merge ("London +12") until you zoom in. Tap a dot for its artists, most records first, or the concert nights there, each a link into the graph. The base map is an outline of the world's countries kept inside the app (`world.json`, Natural Earth 1:50m via world-atlas, public domain, simplified), so it works offline and sends nothing anywhere. Coordinates come from Wikidata with the place lookup (places looked up before get one quick extra pass), and runs by itself when the map opens. Concerts get a dot per venue when Wikidata knows the venue (searched in English and in the phone's language, and only taken when it's in that city), otherwise per city; the city's coordinates come from the setlist.fm export when it has them (re-run the script to add them), otherwise from Wikidata.
- **Quiz** (Explore → Artists, switch from Graph to Quiz; the tab remembers which you had open): two games made fresh from your own collection each time. **Three of a kind**: nine names, three groups of three (members of a band, artists from one place, on one label, in one genre); tap three and Check, three mistakes allowed, "One away" when two of three are right; solved groups take the A, B and C colours and hatchings, and tapping one opens it in the graph. **Five questions**: which band someone played in, where an artist started out, which label your copy is on, what year a record first came out, and which of four doesn't belong; wrong answers come from nearby in the graph so they aren't too easy, and each answer links to the graph. Best score and solved grids are kept on the phone. Questions about members and places get richer as **Fetch artist connections** fills in; label, genre and year questions work straight away.
- Timeline: a vertical timeline by release year with this year on top. Each record hangs off its year, and you can filter by format and genre; the filters stay at the top as you scroll.
- Stats: counts by format, speed, genre and decade, records added per year, how full each storage location is, and, when the Discogs export has them, your ratings, top labels, where you bought your records and what you spent.
- Album covers from MusicBrainz and the Cover Art Archive replace the disc icon on the cards when one is found. They are fetched as you scroll, saved on the phone, and can be switched off in the Data tab.
- Back up and restore with a CSV export that includes every record's location, genre, comment, selection and plays.
- **Full backup** (Data → Collection → Back up): **Save full backup** writes one JSON file with everything Crate keeps on the phone: your records and everything looked up (credits, samples, artist connections, places and events, original years, tempos), concerts and settings. **Restore from backup** asks first, then replaces everything with the backup and reloads; if it doesn't fit in the browser's storage, nothing is changed. Cover images aren't included (they come back by themselves), and neither is the undo copy. The page shows when you last saved one. A backup chosen under Records (CSV) is recognised and restored the same way. The CSV export stays for spreadsheets and Discogs; it leaves out credits, samples and artist connections.
- Data tab: three tabs, and it remembers the one you had open. **Collection** shows the numbers at a glance, both imports (records CSV and concerts CSV), the backup export, and the Undo box right after an import. **Lookups** is one panel: **Update everything** runs every lookup in turn (Discogs details, artist connections, places and events, samples, original years, tempos), one row per lookup with a progress bar and count, a striped bar on the one running, and an overall time estimate; tap a row for its explanation and its own Start. Pausing stops it, and pressing it again picks up where it stopped. Album covers are an on/off switch at the top. **App** has the storage meter, the version and **Check for updates**, and a red **Danger zone** with every button that removes something (each asks first). Storage locations are added and edited on the Storage tab, under **Edit storage locations**.
- Phone storage (Data → App): how much space Crate uses on the phone and how much the browser allows, split into album covers, records, the undo copy, lookups, app files, and concerts and settings. Since version 74 everything is kept in the browser's database (IndexedDB) instead of its 5 MB simple storage, so there is room for far more (gigabytes on most phones); the first start after updating moves the data over by itself and frees the old copy once it's safely moved. Records are saved without their search text and empty fields, which roughly halves their size. If a browser has no database, Crate falls back to the simple storage.
- The MusicBrainz release linked to each Discogs release is remembered on the phone, so covers, original years and tempos don't look it up again.
- Page headings are in Japanese (レコード Records, 収納 Storage, 選択 Selected, 探索 Explore, データ Data) with the English name as a small tag on the right; the tabs at the bottom stay in English.
- Cyberpunk brutalist look: near-black with a faint grid, monospace type, square 2px outlines, one acid-lime accent and magenta for warnings and hard shadows. It follows your phone's light or dark setting; the light version is off-white and black.
- Works offline and can be installed on a phone's home screen.

## Storage locations

Three locations are set up by default and can be edited in the **Data** tab:

| Location | Layout | Holds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kallax shelf | 3 × 4 sections, numbered along each row from the top left | LP, 12", 10" |
| 7" case | One compartment, capacity 200 | 7" singles |
| String Pocket | Shelves layout: 8 units with 3 shelves each, 78 × 20 × 15 cm per shelf, numbered 1–24 from the top | CDs |

You can add more locations with one of three layouts:

- **Grid section**: columns and rows of square sections, drawn as cubes (like a Kallax). Sections are numbered along each row from the top left.
- **Shelves**: units side by side and stacked, each with a number of shelves. Enter the width, height and depth of each shelf in centimetres; the drawing uses those proportions. Shelves are numbered from the top, unit by unit.
- **Drawers**: a cabinet of drawers side by side and stacked. Enter the width, height and depth of each drawer in centimetres. Drawers are numbered along each row from the top left, and inside a drawer records are numbered from the front to the back.

The drawings are wireframe 3D, with depth going up and to the right. A location can't be made smaller than the highest section, shelf or drawer that holds records, so your placements are never lost by an edit.

When you place a record at a position that is already taken, the records from that position onwards shift one place along, as if you slid it in on the shelf (in a drawer: one place further back).

## Use it

1. Export your collection from Discogs as a CSV.
2. Open the app, go to **Data**, and choose the CSV file.
3. Tick the formats you want to track and import.
4. In **Records**, tap a record and choose where it goes.
5. Use the search box to find a record later. **Storage** shows each location and what is in every section.

The import reads the Discogs columns Label, Catalog#, Rating, CollectionFolder, Collection Bought at, Collection Date, Collection Media and Sleeve Condition, Collection Price and Collection Notes. A record without a genre gets one from its collection folder when the folder names a genre ("Electronic (Vinyl)" gives Electronic; artist folders such as "*Kraftwerk" are skipped). It also reads Genre, Comment and Selected columns if the CSV has them (a Selected cell of `Yes` marks a selected record; columns named Starred or Favourite from older exports are read too). Importing again keeps the locations, genres, comments and selections of records that match by Discogs release ID, so you can add records on Discogs, export, and re-import without losing your work. A value in the CSV wins over the app's; an empty cell keeps what the app has. If you own several copies of the same release, they are told apart by the Date Added column, so reordered rows in a Discogs export don't swap their placements. The export also carries Date Added, Last played, Play next, Original release and Plays (every day the record was played, separated by semicolons), so a backup keeps your listening history.

## Concerts from setlist.fm

Crate can show which of your records are by artists you've seen live, using the concerts you've marked as **I was there** on [setlist.fm](https://www.setlist.fm). Records by those artists get a **LIVE ×n** tag, the record sheet lists each concert with date and venue (and the song, if a record's title was played that night), and **Explore → Live** shows your concerts by year with the most-seen artists, venues and concerts per year. Tap a concert to find that artist's records.

setlist.fm doesn't allow web apps to call its API, so the concerts come in as a CSV made on a computer:

1. Get a free API key: on setlist.fm, open your account settings and choose **API**. It's for non-commercial use.
2. On a Mac (or any computer with Python 3 and curl), from this folder run:

   ```bash
   python3 tools/setlistfm_export.py YOUR_SETLISTFM_USERNAME
   ```

   It asks for the API key without showing it (or reads it from the `SETLISTFM_API_KEY` environment variable), and writes `setlistfm-concerts.csv`. The key is never saved.
3. Move the file to your phone and import it in **Data → Collection → Concerts (CSV) → Choose**. Importing again replaces the list, so re-run the script when you've been to more concerts.

Concerts are matched to records by artist name. Accents and a leading "The" are ignored, and a record by "A / B", "A & B" or "A feat. B" matches concerts by either artist. Concerts are stored apart from your collection and aren't in the collection CSV export; keep the setlist.fm CSV as their backup.

## Your data

Everything is stored in your browser's own storage (its database) on your own device. Nothing of your collection is uploaded, apart from the lookups: for album covers and original years a record's Discogs number, and sometimes its artist and title, are sent to MusicBrainz (covers can be switched off), and **Fetch details from Discogs** sends each Discogs number to Discogs, and this repository contains only the app, not your collection. Browser storage can be cleared, so use **Data → Export CSV** now and then to keep a backup. Don't commit your Discogs CSV to a public repository.

Storage is separate for each web address. If you move from a local file to the hosted version, export a CSV first and import it in the new place.

## Install it on a phone

The app needs to be served over https to be installable. GitHub Pages does this for free:

1. In the repository, go to **Settings → Pages**.
2. Under **Build and deployment**, choose **Deploy from a branch**, select the `main` branch and the `/ (root)` folder, and save.
3. Open the address GitHub gives you in Chrome on your phone.
4. Open the menu and choose **Add to Home screen** or **Install app**.

It then opens full screen like an app and works offline.

## Updating

The app checks for a newer version when it opens, a few seconds after it starts, and whenever you switch back to it. If the version on GitHub is newer, it clears its saved app files and reloads by itself. Your records, locations and album covers are not touched. The **Data** tab shows the running version at the bottom, with a **Check for updates** button.

When you make a new release, raise `APP_VERSION` (and the date) near the top of the script in `index.html`, and change the cache name in `sw.js` to match.
